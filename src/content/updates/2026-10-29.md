Most of today sat on one question: what should happen when someone edits a piece of content that has already been approved. The answer took three passes to get right, and the last one is the one that matters.

## For our partners

1. Editing an approved piece of content now sends the new version back for approval instead of leaving the old version pinned.
2. When the person saving is also the person who approves, the save simply approves the new version. When the client approves, the client gets asked.
3. Active Websites lists built sites only, in three states: Staged, Published and Live.
4. The Content Plan in the client portal links each title to the published page or to the review waiting on the client.
5. The partner API gained websites, website logins, plans read by type, services and packages, with matching tools for an agency's own assistant.

### 1. An edit after approval asks again

Approval used to pin a piece for good. If a client approved by mistake and asked for changes the same afternoon, the revised version had nowhere to go: the piece stayed in the publishing lane pointing at the old words. Now a version saved after an approval opens its own approval on that version, and the previous approval stops counting. The backstop is unchanged: nothing unapproved is ever published.

Saving a piece with nothing changed since the approval is refused, with a note to edit it first.

### 2. Who approves decides what a save does

Sending everything back for approval was right for client-reviewed content and wrong for partner-reviewed content, where a member would have ended up asking themselves for permission in their own inbox. So the rule reads the approver. A member saving an edit on a campaign their agency approves gets "Saved and approved" and the new version ships. Where content review belongs to the client, the save asks the client and the piece shows as waiting. A save made by us always asks whoever approves.

### 3. Active Websites and Website Onboards

Active Websites was showing sites still in the build wizard, which made it read as a duplicate of Website Onboards. Sites still being set up now stay on Onboards. Everything on Active Websites is built, and each row says how far it has got: Staged (on its staging address), Published (on its tidysites.dev address) or Live (serving on the client's own domain). Each badge explains itself on hover.

### 5. More of the platform reachable from your own tools

The API can now read a client's websites, including what each site runs on and which services are sold on it. Website logins are a record of their own: add one, update one, rotate a password in place, or mark one as not working with a note, and your team is told when a login goes bad. Plans read the way the planning tab shows them, one type at a time, with approve and send-feedback going through the same door the page uses. Services follow the words the app uses: the SEO campaign pauses or ends, and archiving a client is its own separate mark. There is also a door for reading the package shelf, so starting a campaign no longer means fetching an identifier out of the app by hand.

On the questions you ask us, the partner view drops our side of the record and shows the thread: each open question now carries the answered questions it follows.

## Behind the scenes

The content approval rule took three merges in one day. The first opened a fresh approval whenever a newer version existed but left it to a button that people forget. The second made the save itself do it, which kept the dialog's promise. The third read who the approver is, because sending a partner's own edit back to the partner is a loop, not a safeguard. The rule that survived is short: an edit after approval needs approval, and if you are the approver, saving is approving.

The hosting side is a bigger rebuild. We have been running WordPress hosting through a third-party control panel, and we are moving it in-house: a server list and a site list, each opening to its own pages for caching, certificates, PHP, logs, backups and the rest. Certificates were the part worth being careful about. The fleet reads back what each server is actually serving, not what a record claims, and renewals are queued automatically from thirty days out, with an alert at fourteen days and a louder one at seven. The export we read on the way in showed a few hundred certificates expiring in December and January, which is the kind of thing you want to find in October.

Two smaller threads. Our agents are told to honour a partner's switch for unattended work, and the sweep that publishes Business Profile posts now reads that switch like every other path does. And our own alerting stopped lumping every failed agent run under one heading: a signed-out account, a refusal from the model and an agent someone switched off are three different problems and now read as three different alerts.
