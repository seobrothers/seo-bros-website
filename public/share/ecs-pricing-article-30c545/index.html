How Much Does Commercial Cleaning Cost? A 2026 Pricing Guide

Draft v1 · scheduled for August 31, 2026

If you are budgeting for a cleaning service, the honest answer is that commercial cleaning pricing has a wide range, because no two facilities create the same amount of work. A 2,000 square foot office cleaned twice a week is a very different job than a 40,000 square foot medical building cleaned nightly.

That said, the ranges below reflect how most reputable commercial cleaning companies price their work in 2026, and by the end of this guide you should be able to estimate a realistic budget for your own facility before you ever request a quote.

The Three Ways Commercial Cleaning Is Priced

Most providers quote one of three ways, and knowing which model fits your situation helps you compare bids apples to apples.

Hourly rates are common for small spaces, one-time jobs, and situations where the scope is hard to predict. Typical rates run $30 to $60 per cleaner, per hour, with higher rates in major metros and for specialized work like disinfection or post-construction cleanup.

Per-square-foot pricing is the standard for recurring service in offices and larger facilities. Expect roughly $0.05 to $0.20 per square foot, per visit for routine janitorial work. Lower-traffic office space sits at the bottom of that range; restrooms, kitchens, and medical areas push it up.

Flat monthly contracts are what most businesses actually end up with for recurring service. The provider walks your facility, builds a scope of work, and quotes a fixed monthly price. For a small office cleaned two or three times a week, monthly pricing often lands between $400 and $1,500 per month. Larger facilities with nightly service can run several thousand per month.

Why Property Type Changes the Price So Much

The property type is doing most of the work in that table, so it is worth understanding why some buildings cost more per square foot than others. In general, price follows three things: how dirty the space gets between visits, how careful the cleaning has to be, and how much of the square footage actually needs hands-on attention.

Standard offices are the baseline. Predictable dirt, standard surfaces, and low risk. Every other property type is priced up or down from here.

Warehouses often cost less per square foot, but more than people expect overall. A huge open floor sounds cheap to clean, and on a per-square-foot basis it usually is, because there is less detail work per thousand feet. But warehouses add costs offices never see: machine scrubbing of concrete floors, high dusting of racking and beams (often requiring lifts), dock areas that track in grime daily, and safety protocols for working around forklifts and inventory. The result is a lower rate applied to a very large area, with periodic big-ticket tasks layered on top. That is why warehouse cleaning is usually quoted as a custom scope rather than a rate card.

Medical and dental offices sit at the top of the range. Disinfection protocols, cross-contamination controls, biohazard handling, and documentation requirements mean more labor hours and more expensive products for the same square footage.

Gyms and fitness centers run high because of frequency and touchpoints. Sweat, shared equipment, locker rooms, and showers demand daily attention, and members notice lapses immediately.

Restaurants and food service price above offices because health-code standards apply to dining areas, restrooms, and front-of-house surfaces, and grease migrates everywhere.

Retail sits near office rates but adds glass, entry floors that take a beating from weather and foot traffic, and the pressure of being customer-facing every open hour.

The takeaway: square footage alone tells a provider very little. A 30,000 square foot warehouse and a 30,000 square foot medical center might differ in monthly price by a factor of three or more.

7 Factors That Move Your Price

When two similar-looking offices get very different quotes, one or more of these is usually the reason.

  1. Cleaning frequency. Counterintuitively, more frequent service usually lowers your per-visit price. A facility cleaned five nights a week stays maintained, so each visit is faster than a once-a-week deep reset.
  2. Square footage and layout. Total area matters, but so does layout. Open floor plans clean faster than warrens of private offices, and multi-floor buildings add time.
  3. Restroom and kitchen count. These are the most labor-intensive areas in any building. A quote for a facility with ten restrooms will never match one with two.
  4. Foot traffic. A lobby that sees 500 visitors a day needs more attention than one that sees 20, even at identical square footage.
  5. Scope of work. Basic janitorial service (trash, vacuuming, restrooms, surfaces) is the baseline. Add-ons like floor stripping and waxing, carpet extraction, window cleaning, or electrostatic disinfection are typically quoted separately.
  6. Industry requirements. Medical facilities, food service, daycare, and government buildings carry compliance requirements that add training, documentation, and product costs.
  7. Local labor market. Cleaning is a labor business. Prices in San Francisco or New York run meaningfully higher than the national ranges above; smaller markets run lower.

Why the Cheapest Bid Is Usually the Most Expensive

A quote well below market usually means one of three things: the provider underestimated the scope and will cut corners to protect their margin, they are underpaying or under-insuring their staff, or the low price is an introductory rate that climbs after you have committed.

Corner-cutting shows up slowly, in dusty vents, streaked glass, and restrooms that get a wipe instead of a clean, and by the time it is obvious, you are stuck mid-contract. That is one reason to prefer providers that earn the business month to month instead of locking you into long terms. It is also why an unusually low bid deserves more scrutiny than an unusually high one: ask exactly what is in scope, how many labor hours the price assumes, and what happens to the rate at renewal.

An office cleaning program priced correctly should feel invisible: the space is simply always ready for employees and clients, and you spend zero management time on it.

How to Get an Accurate Quote

Ballpark ranges get you a budget. An accurate number requires a walkthrough, because square footage alone misses everything in the factor list above. To get quotes you can actually compare:

  • Request a written scope of work, not just a price. Task lists and frequencies should be itemized so you can line bids up side by side.
  • Ask how the price was built. A provider who can explain their labor-hour math has actually thought about your building.
  • Confirm insurance and vetting. Liability coverage and background-checked staff are table stakes, and they are part of why professional pricing is what it is.
  • Ask about contract terms. Month-to-month arrangements keep the provider accountable; multi-year lock-ins protect the provider, not you.

The Bottom Line

For most offices, realistic 2026 budgeting looks like $30 to $60 per hour for ad hoc work, $0.05 to $0.20 per square foot per visit for recurring janitorial service, and $400 to $1,500 per month for a small office on a two-or-three-visit weekly schedule. Your exact number comes down to frequency, layout, restrooms, traffic, and scope.
